Australian small businesses · 2022–2026

Small in size. Big on risk.

Almost every business in Australia is small. Most have no staff at all. This page follows them from start-up to survival, and to the point where some of them fail.

01

Who they are: a nation of one-person businesses

In this story, a small business is one with fewer than 20 employees, the ABS definition. A non-employing business has no staff: think a tradie, a consultant or a delivery driver working alone.

Composition · June 2026

Out of every 100 businesses, 65 have no employees

Each square is one in every hundred Australian businesses. Only 3 squares represent firms with 20 or more staff.

Together, the three small-business groups make up 97.4% of all 2,814,778 actively trading businesses.

Data: ABS, Counts of Australian Businesses (CABEE), Jul 2022–Jun 2026, Table 13.

Change · 2022 → 2026

Solo operators are taking a bigger slice

Between June 2022 and June 2026 the non-employing share rose from 59.9% to 64.6%. The 1–4 employee share fell from 28.6% to 24.5%.

Larger groups barely moved. The growth in Australian business is coming from people working on their own.

Data: ABS CABEE, Table 13. Share = group count ÷ all businesses at each date. Axis starts at zero.

Flows · 1–4 employee businesses

393,004 new micro-businesses opened, yet the group still shrank

Start with June 2022, add entries, subtract exits and businesses that changed size. The bar on the right is June 2026.

Entries outnumbered exits by 125,563. But 163,420 surviving businesses moved to another size group, for example by hiring more staff or letting all staff go. Net result: 37,857 fewer businesses with 1–4 employees.

Data: ABS CABEE, Table 13, four financial years 2022–23 to 2025–26 added together. “Moved to another size” is the ABS “net movement of surviving businesses”.

Legal structure · 2022–23 to 2025–26

Sole traders churn fastest; partnerships are fading

Each line traces one legal structure over four years. Points below the dashed line mean more businesses started than closed.

Sole proprietors have the highest entry rate (23.0%) and exit rate (19.9%): easy to start, easy to stop.

Partnerships sit above the line every year. Their number fell from 231,843 to 196,015.

Data: ABS CABEE, Table 10. Entry rate = entries ÷ businesses at start of year. Click a legend item to focus a line.

02

Where they are: business is densest in the south-east

Raw counts would simply show where people live. So this map divides businesses by population to compare states fairly.

Choropleth map · June 2026

NSW and Victoria have about 108 businesses per 1,000 residents

That is two-thirds more than the Northern Territory (65 per 1,000). Tasmania and the ACT also sit below 80.

Hover a state to see its business count, growth and insolvencies.

The map uses an Albers equal-area projection, so state sizes are not distorted the way they are on a web (Mercator) map.

Data: ABS CABEE Table 4 (businesses, June 2026) ÷ ABS National, State and Territory Population, March 2026 (estimated resident population). The four colour classes were set at natural gaps in the eight values.

03

Who survives: the smaller you start, the shorter you last

The ABS follows the same group of businesses year after year. “Surviving” means still actively trading, not necessarily making a profit.

Survival heatmap · businesses trading in June 2022

After four years, only 55% of businesses with no employees were still trading

Read across each row: how many of the businesses trading in June 2022 were still going each following June. Darker means more survived.

Survival rises with every step up in size: from 55.3% for non-employing businesses to 89.3% for those with 200+ staff. This shows an association, not proof that hiring causes survival.

Data: ABS CABEE, Table 15. Size is fixed at the June 2022 starting point.

Dumbbell chart · new businesses

New solo businesses lose most ground after their first year

Of the 297,302 non-employing businesses that started in 2022–23, 73% were still trading a year later, but only 45% three years later.

The longer the bar, the bigger the drop. It is longest for the smallest businesses.

Data: ABS CABEE, Table 16 (entries survival). Axis starts at zero.

04

Who fails, and why

Most closures are quiet: an owner simply stops trading. When a company cannot pay its debts, an external administrator (such as a liquidator) is appointed. These insolvencies are recorded by ASIC, the corporate regulator.

Calendar heatmap · monthly

Insolvencies have stayed near record levels for two years

Each cell is one month. Monthly counts climbed from about 400 in 2021–22 to more than 1,200 in 2024–25.

2024–25 had 14,722 companies enter insolvency and 2025–26 had 14,153, about three times the 4,912 of 2021–22. August 2026 (1,808) is the highest month shown; recent months are provisional.

Data: ASIC Insolvency Statistics Series 1, Table 1.3 (companies entering external administration for the first time), published 12 October 2026, data to 27 September 2026.

Proportional symbol map · 2025–26

Most failures happen where most businesses are

Circle area shows how many companies entered insolvency. New South Wales (5,367) and Victoria (4,100) dominate.

Data: ASIC Series 1, Table 1.3, by principal place of business, FY2025–26.

Hexagon tile map · 2025–26

But relative to size, the ACT is most at risk

Every state gets an equal hexagon, so small territories are not hidden. Colour shows insolvencies per 10,000 businesses.

The ACT (68) and NSW (57) have the highest rates; Western Australia (30) the lowest.

Data: ASIC Series 1 ÷ ABS CABEE Table 4 (June 2026). ASIC counts companies only; the ABS count includes all business types.

Butterfly chart · 2024–25

Hospitality is 4% of businesses but 17% of company failures

Left: each industry's share of all businesses. Right: its share of company failures reported by liquidators. Bold industries fail more often than their size would suggest.

Construction is 17.0% of businesses and 24.6% of failures. Of failed companies whose size was known, 79% had fewer than five full-time staff and 95% had fewer than 20.

Data: ABS CABEE Table 1 (June 2025) and ASIC Insolvency Statistics Series 3.1, Tables 3.1.1–3.1.2 (initial external administrators' reports, 1 July 2024–30 June 2025). Labour hire is merged into admin & support. ASIC lists some trades (e.g. electricians, plumbers) under utilities, so small industries are not perfectly comparable.

Bump chart · 2021–22 to 2025–26

The same three industries top the failure list every year, while health care is climbing

Each line is one industry's rank by the number of companies entering insolvency (1 = most). Hover a line for counts.

Hospitality has been second every year behind construction. Health care & social assistance rose from 14th to 10th, with failures up from 73 to 464 a year.

Data: ASIC Insolvency Statistics Series 1, Table 1.2 (companies entering external administration for the first time, by industry), published 12 October 2026. Ranked by count, so large industries rank higher.

Interactive bar chart · 2024–25

Running out of cash is the most common reason companies fail

Liquidators can name several causes per company. Pick an industry; the dark tick shows the average for all industries.

Across all industries, 57% of reports named inadequate cash flow. In hospitality, poor economic conditions (40%) were named far more often than average (26%).

Data: ASIC Insolvency Statistics Series 3.1, Table 3.1.3 (nominated causes of failure, 2024–25). Seven most-named causes shown.

What this means

  1. Australia's business growth is driven by people working alone, and they are the least likely to last.
  2. Getting through the first three years, and to the first hire, is where survival rates diverge.
  3. Company failures have nearly tripled since 2021–22, concentrated in very small firms, construction and hospitality. Cash flow is the warning sign to watch.
